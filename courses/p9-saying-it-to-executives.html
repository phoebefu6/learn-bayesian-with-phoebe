Learn Bayesian with Phoebe · Practitioner session 9 of 10

Saying it to executives

Eight sessions produced a posterior. Nobody upstairs will ever see it. What they see is a paragraph, and the paragraph is where most Bayesian work quietly turns back into a point estimate with a confident adjective. Tonight is the paragraph: four parts, in a fixed order, each one a number the analysis actually supports. The estimate. The interval, with its level and its kind said out loud. The probability of the thing they care about. And what is at stake if it is wrong. Northgate's eighteen vouchers and Westfield's A/B test are the two worked cases, because one of them has almost no data and the other has a decision riding on it, and the same sentence has to carry both without lying in either direction.

Part 0

What the sentence is for

A posterior is a distribution, and an executive is going to act on one number. The sentence is the bridge, and it can fail in two directions. It can hide the width, so that 0.100 from eighteen vouchers reads like 0.142 from nine hundred. Or it can hide the stake, so that a 97.5 percent probability sounds like a decision when it is only half of one. Leader session 6 is the mirror of tonight: it says what a leader should ask for in writing. Tonight is how the practitioner writes it so that nothing has to be asked.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A four-part sentence you can fill from any posterior: estimate, interval with level and kind, probability, stake. The Northgate version, "0.100, an 89 percent probability between 0.020 and 0.222, equal-tailed, and eighteen vouchers cannot pin it tighter." The Westfield version, "a 97.5 percent probability B is better, an expected cost of 0.0005 if wrong, and 2 wrong stops in 200 replays." A list of five things the sentence must never do. And a Python function that formats all of it from a Beta posterior or a DataTree, so the paragraph is generated from the same numbers it describes.

LiveFour parts, one breath4 min▶

Here is Northgate under the flat prior, session 2's first row, said the way it should be said. "The best estimate of Northgate's redemption rate is 0.100. There is an 89 percent probability it lies between 0.020 and 0.222 (equal-tailed). Eighteen vouchers cannot pin it tighter than that." Three sentences, four jobs. The estimate is the posterior mean, not the observed 0.056, because the observed rate is one redemption in eighteen and the posterior mean is what that evidence supports once the two pseudo-observations of the flat prior are counted. The interval carries its level, 89 percent, and its kind, equal-tailed, because either word missing makes the range unreproducible. The stake is the last sentence: the width is the news, and the reader is told so rather than left to notice.

The fourth part, the probability of the thing they care about, is absent at Northgate on purpose. No decision hangs on Northgate alone tonight, so there is no "the thing they care about" to put a probability on. When there is one, as at Westfield, it goes in as its own sentence with its own stake. A sentence that includes a probability nobody asked for is padding; a sentence that omits the one they did ask for is the real failure, and it is the more common of the two.

Real world

Halloway's regional manager received Northgate as "0.100" in a table of twelve outlets, each with three decimals and no widths. Kingsway sat at 0.209, Northgate at 0.100, and the table looked like a league. The interval that would have stopped the closure review, 0.020 to 0.222, was in the analyst's notebook the whole time. The paragraph never carried it upstairs, and a paragraph that does not carry the width is a point estimate wearing a Bayesian badge.

Self-studyThe four parts, named3 min▶
  • The estimate. The posterior mean, stated as "the best estimate" and never alone. Northgate 0.100 under the flat prior; 0.124 under partial pooling from session 6. Which one you report depends on the prior line below, and the reader is told which.
  • The interval, with its level and its kind. "There is an 89 percent probability it lies between 0.020 and 0.222 (equal-tailed)." The level is 89 because that is what ArviZ 1 reports by default; the kind is equal-tailed because that is how ArviZ 1 cuts it, at the 5.5 and 94.5 percent quantiles. Both words, every time.
  • The probability of the thing they care about. Only when a decision hangs on the number. At Westfield it is P(B > A) = 0.975 at n = 100 per arm. It is never the interval restated; it is the probability of the action being right.
  • The stake if wrong. At Westfield, the expected cost of choosing B is 0.0005 in redemption rate. At Northgate, where there is no decision, the stake is what the data cannot do: eighteen vouchers cannot pin the rate tighter than 0.020 to 0.222. A probability without a stake is not a decision, session 7's whole point, and the sentence inherits it.
Part 1 · covers the four-part sentence and what it can promise

The sentence, with Northgate slotted in

LiveWhat the sentence can promise, and what it cannot4 min▶

The flat-prior sentence for Northgate is true and nearly useless: 0.020 to 0.222 would support closing the outlet or doubling its budget with equal ease, and the sentence is honest about that in its last line. Session 6 gives a better estimate. Partial pooling across the twelve outlets learned kappa = 116, weights Northgate's own month at 0.13, and puts the outlet at 0.124. The pooled sentence starts differently: "Under partial pooling across Halloway's twelve outlets, the best estimate of Northgate's redemption rate is 0.124, leaning 0.87 on the other eleven." Then its interval, cut the same way, with the same two words. Then the same closing stake, because eighteen vouchers are still eighteen vouchers.

Here is what neither sentence can promise. Northgate's true rate is 0.192. The flat interval contains it, the pooled estimate halved the error from 0.136 to 0.068 and is still under it. An 89 percent interval promises that 89 percent of the posterior's mass sits inside the range; it does not promise the truth is there, and a sentence that says "the rate is between 0.020 and 0.222" has dropped the word probability and made a promise the arithmetic never made. Say "there is an 89 percent probability". Whether your 89 percent intervals turn out to contain the truth 89 percent of the time, measured across many such sentences, is a calibration question, and learn-decision-intelligence-with-phoebe sessions 02 and 04 own it. The sentence's job is to be the thing that audit can be run on.

Self-studyWhy "89 percent equal-tailed" is said out loud3 min▶

Until ArviZ 1 the default summary interval was a 94 percent HDI, the narrowest range holding 94 percent of the posterior. ArviZ 1 changed both halves at once: stats.ci_prob is now 0.89 and stats.ci_kind is now "eti", an equal-tailed interval cut at the 5.5 and 94.5 percent quantiles. The maintainers' stated reason is that 0.89 produces intervals with lower variability and therefore more stable summaries. The argument for the number is theirs to make. The consequence for the sentence is not optional.

A third-party repository documented two notebooks whose intervals silently became equal-tailed on upgrade while the prose beside them still said HDI. The code ran, the numbers changed, the words did not. A sentence that says "the interval is 0.020 to 0.222" cannot be reproduced, because a reader does not know whether to cut 89 or 94, equal-tailed or highest-density, and each choice gives a different range from the same posterior. A sentence that says "89 percent, equal-tailed" can be reproduced by anyone with the draws. That is the whole reason both words are in the template, and it is why the keyword in ArviZ 1 is now prob, set explicitly, rather than a default trusted.

Self-studyCredible against confidence, once2 min▶

The Northgate interval is a credible interval, and it supports the sentence "there is an 89 percent probability the rate is in here", because the posterior is a distribution over the rate and the interval holds 89 percent of it. A confidence interval is a different object: a statement about the procedure that produced it, across repeated samples, and it does not support that sentence about this rate. Executives hear the Bayesian sentence whichever interval they are shown, which is exactly why the Bayesian one has to be labelled and the other one has to be handed on. The frequentist machinery, what a confidence interval does claim and how it is built, is learn-statistics-with-phoebe session b7, and nothing about it is derived here. The contrast is the only thing tonight needs.

Part 2 · covers the A/B paragraph and the five things it must not do

The paragraph with a decision in it

10 min live

Westfield's A/B test from session 7 is the case where a probability goes upstairs and somebody acts on it. The same result can be written as a paragraph that misleads without a single false number, or as one that can be argued with. The figure puts them side by side, with every misleading move marked.

LiveProbability and stake, in the same sentence4 min▶

At n = 100 per arm, Westfield's test reads A 0.100, B 0.200. P(B > A) is 0.975, the expected loss of choosing B is 0.0005 in redemption rate, and the rule fixed before the experiment, stop when the expected loss of the chosen arm falls below 0.001, says stop and choose B. The executive sentence carries the probability and the stake together: "There is a 97.5 percent probability B is better; if we are wrong, the expected cost is 0.0005 in redemption rate." The semicolon is doing real work. Split into two sentences, the first one gets quoted alone. Session 7 showed why that matters: at n = 50 the probability was already 0.915 and the rule said keep going, because the expected loss was 0.0029, three times the threshold. A probability alone would have stopped fifty customers too early.

Then the honesty clause, in the same paragraph and never in an appendix. Across 200 seeded replays of the same experiment, stopping at n = 100 by the same rule, 23 would have stopped and 2 of those 200 would have stopped on the wrong arm. Write it as a count: "Run 200 times, this rule stopped on the wrong arm 2 times in 200." It is a 1 percent wrong-stop rate at that sample size, it is cheap, and it is not free. A reader who gets the 0.975 and the 0.0005 without the 2 in 200 has been told the price of the decision and not the odds of having bought the wrong thing. The reason looking early and often would inflate that count is learn-experimentation-with-phoebe session b6's argument. The paragraph's job is to report the measured rate and stop there.

Self-studyFive things the sentence must never do4 min▶
  • Report a point estimate without its interval. "Northgate is at 0.100" is the sentence that flagged the outlet for closure. The interval was 0.020 to 0.222 and would have ended the conversation.
  • Report a probability without its stake. "97.5 percent probability B is better" is half a decision. The other half is 0.0005, and at n = 50 the half you would have quoted was 0.915 with a stake of 0.0029 behind it, which the rule rejected.
  • Say "significant". The word belongs to a procedure this sentence did not run and would be read as a verdict from it. Say the probability and the stake instead; they are what you have.
  • Say the prior "washed out". It was outvoted, and a hundred votes take a long time to outvote. The Wrong prior, Beta(40, 60), worth 100 pseudo-observations, still moves Westfield's estimate by 0.026 at n = 900: posterior mean 0.167 against 0.142 under the flat prior. Session 2's number, and the reason the prior disclosure line below exists.
  • Promise there is no peeking problem. The expected-loss rule controls the cost of a wrong decision, not the frequency of one, and the 2 in 200 is the frequency. State the count and hand the argument to learn-experimentation-with-phoebe b6. A paragraph that says "we could stop whenever we liked" has made a claim the bench measured and contradicted.
Self-studyThe prior disclosure line3 min▶

Every executive paragraph carries one more line, usually first: which prior, worth how many pseudo-observations, and why. "Flat, Beta(1, 1), worth 2 pseudo-observations, because we had no defensible history for a new outlet." Or "Informed, Beta(12, 88), worth 100 pseudo-observations, from the chain-wide history." Or "Learned by partial pooling across the twelve outlets, kappa 116, which weights Northgate's own month at 0.13." Two numbers and a provenance. The line exists because the estimate depends on it: Northgate reads 0.100, 0.088, 0.110 or 0.347 under session 2's four priors, and an executive who has not been told which is which has been handed a number with a hidden author. The argument about how a prior should be chosen, the three positions and the shared ground of a prior predictive check, is leader session 5's, and the disclosure line is where its conclusion lands in one clause.

The line also catches the prior that should never have shipped. The Wrong prior, Beta(40, 60), predicts roughly 40 percent redemption. Halloway has never observed above 0.225 at any outlet. A prior predictive check, session 8, fails it before a single voucher is counted, and a disclosure line that says "Beta(40, 60), worth 100 pseudo-observations" fails it in the reader's head for the same reason. What the line cannot do is tell you whether the intervals it introduces are honest over many forecasts. That measurement, calibration over a track record, is learn-decision-intelligence-with-phoebe sessions 02 and 04.

Part 3 · build-along

Generate the paragraph from the posterior

16 min build-along

Laptop out. The sentence is a template and the numbers come from the posterior, so the sentence should be produced by code that reads the posterior, not typed from memory beside it. Two functions: one that formats the four-part sentence from a Beta posterior or a DataTree, one that formats the A/B sentence from P(B > A) and the expected loss. Canon numbers are in the comments so the output can be checked.

Pull the mean and the 5.5 and 94.5 percent quantiles. From a scipy Beta posterior use ppf, which is exact. From a DataTree, reach the group as dt["posterior"].dataset and take quantiles over the draws. Never let a default choose the level; the two numbers 0.055 and 0.945 are written in the code so the sentence and the interval cannot drift apart.

Format the sentence with the level and the kind as literals. "89 percent" and "equal-tailed" are in the f-string, not looked up. If somebody changes the quantiles they have to change the words in the same line, which is the property the third-party notebooks lacked.

Check Northgate. Beta(2, 18) after one redemption in eighteen under the flat prior. The mean must print 0.100 and the interval 0.020 to 0.222. If it does not, the posterior is wrong, not the sentence.

Format the A/B sentence with the stake in the same string. P(B > A) and the expected loss of choosing B are Monte Carlo over the two posteriors. The 2 in 200 comes from session 7's replay and is passed in, because a single run cannot produce it. It goes into the same paragraph by construction.

Read the output as an executive would. Estimate, interval with both words, probability with its stake, the count of wrong stops. If any of the four is missing from the printed paragraph, the function is wrong, and that is the point of generating it.

★ Run it now (Python) - the four-part sentence from a posterior# The four-part sentence, generated from the posterior it describes. # Works on a scipy Beta posterior (sessions 1 to 3) or on the DataTree # that pm.sample() returns (session 5). PyMC 6.3.2, ArviZ 1.3.0, Python 3.12 or later. import numpy as np from scipy import stats LEVEL = (0.055, 0.945) # 89 percent, equal-tailed: the ArviZ 1 default (ci_prob 0.89, ci_kind "eti") def summarise(posterior, var="rate"): """Mean and the 5.5 / 94.5 percent quantiles, exact for a Beta, from draws for a DataTree.""" if hasattr(posterior, "ppf"): # a scipy Beta: exact quantiles lo, hi = posterior.ppf(LEVEL) return float(posterior.mean()), float(lo), float(hi) draws = posterior["posterior"].dataset[var].values.ravel() # a DataTree from pm.sample() lo, hi = np.quantile(draws, LEVEL) return float(draws.mean()), float(lo), float(hi) def sentence(posterior, outlet, stake, var="rate"): mean, lo, hi = summarise(posterior, var) return ( f"The best estimate of {outlet}'s redemption rate is {mean:.3f}. " f"There is an 89 percent probability it lies between {lo:.3f} and {hi:.3f} (equal-tailed). " f"{stake}" ) # Northgate under the flat prior: Beta(1 + 1, 1 + 17) = Beta(2, 18) northgate = stats.beta(2, 18) print(sentence(northgate, "Northgate", "Eighteen vouchers cannot pin it tighter than that.")) # The best estimate of Northgate's redemption rate is 0.100. # There is an 89 percent probability it lies between 0.020 and 0.222 (equal-tailed). # Eighteen vouchers cannot pin it tighter than that. # From session 5's Westfield model the call is the same, with the DataTree in place of the Beta: # print(sentence(dt, "Westfield", "Nine hundred vouchers; the width is not the news here."))
★ Run it now (Python) - the A/B sentence with its stake# The A/B sentence for Westfield at n = 100 per arm, Bench C. # Observed A 10 of 100, B 20 of 100; flat Beta(1, 1) priors; 20,000 paired draws. rng = np.random.default_rng(1) a = stats.beta(1 + 10, 1 + 90).rvs(20_000, random_state=rng) b = stats.beta(1 + 20, 1 + 80).rvs(20_000, random_state=rng) p_b_better = float(np.mean(b > a)) # 0.975 on the bench; yours lands within Monte Carlo noise loss_if_b = float(np.mean(np.maximum(a - b, 0.0))) # 0.0005 on the bench: the stake of choosing B loss_if_a = float(np.mean(np.maximum(b - a, 0.0))) # 0.0989 on the bench: the stake of choosing A THRESHOLD = 0.001 # fixed before the data, session 7 decision = "stop and choose B" if loss_if_b < THRESHOLD else "keep going" def ab_sentence(p, loss, decision, wrong_stops, replays): return ( f"There is a {100 * p:.1f} percent probability B is better; " f"if we are wrong, the expected cost is {loss:.4f} in redemption rate. " f"The rule says {decision}. " f"Run {replays} times, this rule stopped on the wrong arm {wrong_stops} times in {replays}." ) # 2 in 200 is the measured wrong-stop rate from session 7's seeded replay at n = 100. # A single run cannot produce it, so it is passed in, and it lands in the same paragraph. print(ab_sentence(p_b_better, loss_if_b, decision, wrong_stops=2, replays=200)) # There is a 97.5 percent probability B is better; if we are wrong, the expected cost is # 0.0005 in redemption rate. The rule says stop and choose B. # Run 200 times, this rule stopped on the wrong arm 2 times in 200.

If the Northgate line does not print 0.100 and 0.020 to 0.222, stop. Those three numbers are session 2's first row and they are exact, so a different output is a wrong posterior or a wrong quantile pair, never a rounding matter. The A/B line is Monte Carlo and will sit within noise of 0.975 and 0.0005; if it says 0.915 and 0.0029 you have loaded n = 50, the case where the rule said keep going.

Check yourself

Three questions

An analyst writes: "Northgate's redemption rate is 0.100 (0.020 to 0.222)." Which parts of the sentence are missing, and why does the omission matter?

Two words and one clause. "89 percent" and "equal-tailed" make the range reproducible from the draws; a third party documented notebooks whose intervals changed kind on upgrade while the prose did not. The stake at Northgate is what the data cannot do, and it is said rather than implied. A probability of a difference belongs only where a decision hangs on the number, which is not the case for Northgate alone.

The executive summary for Westfield reads, in full: "There is a 97.5 percent probability B is better. Recommendation: roll out B." What must be added before it goes upstairs?

A probability without a stake is half a decision; at n = 50 the probability was already 0.915 and the rule said keep going because the loss was 0.0029. The 2 in 200 is the measured frequency of a wrong stop and it goes beside the estimate, never in an appendix. "Significant" names a procedure that was not run.

A paragraph on Westfield's estimate, n = 900, says "at this sample size the prior washed out". What is wrong with the sentence?

Session 2's Westfield row: flat 0.142, weak 0.141, informed 0.139, wrong 0.167. A hundred pseudo-observations against nine hundred real ones still carry a tenth of the answer. The disclosure line, which prior, worth how many, and why, is what lets a reader see how much of the estimate is belief.
